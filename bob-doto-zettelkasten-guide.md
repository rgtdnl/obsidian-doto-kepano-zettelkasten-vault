# Bob Doto's Zettelkasten: Step-by-Step Guide

Source: *A System for Writing* (2024), Bob Doto. A practical, beginner-friendly guide to how the author sets up, builds, and maintains his zettelkasten, with all the detail from the book.

---

## The big picture: his 5-step formula

Doto's whole system reduces to five steps:

1. **Capture** ideas in the form of fleeting and/or reference notes.
2. **Transform** these captures into individual main notes.
3. **Connect** the ideas recorded in the main notes.
4. **Track** developing trains of thought in hub notes, structure notes, and indexes.
5. **Turn** these trains of thought into writing.

Everything below is those five steps expanded. The mindset that makes it different from ordinary note-taking:

- The zettelkasten is organized **bottom-up**. There are no pre-set categories, no topical folders, no hierarchy. Structure emerges from the relationships you create between ideas.
- It is a **practice**, not a project. It takes months or years to feel natural. Luhmann himself called it a form of "controlled chaos."
- Notes are objects; ideas are what live inside them. The value of an idea is determined by its relationships to other ideas, not by where it's filed.

## Phase 0: Setup (what to create before your first note)

Doto's start-up checklist, in his order:

1. **Something portable to capture thoughts.** A folded sheet of paper, a pocket notebook, or a notes app on your phone. It must be intentionally set aside for capturing thoughts as they arise.
2. **An "Inbox".** A physical wire basket or a digital folder named `Inbox`. All fleeting notes land here until processed. The inbox keeps raw thoughts out of the zettelkasten, so the slip box stays a pleasant place to visit.
3. **A "Sleeping" folder.** A second compartment/folder for notes that keep getting passed over during processing but still feel relevant. Check it every few months.
4. **The zettelkasten itself.** Digital: a text editor with linking capabilities (Obsidian, Notion, Roam, The Archive, Zettlr), with a folder titled `Zettelkasten`, containing two subfolders: **`Reference Notes`** and **`Main Notes`**. Paper: a stack of index cards plus a slip box (a long narrow box, card-catalog style) with a reference compartment and a main compartment.
5. **Capture slips for reading.** Blank paper slips (his preference: blank greeting cards from stationery stores) that fit inside a book, kept where you read. For digital reading: a read-later app with note-taking.

Two setup rules he states plainly:

- **Do not import your old notes.** Start fresh. Bring old notes in only as needed, later.
- Keep **one** zettelkasten for all ideas you want to see interact. Don't split personal and study boxes if you want those ideas to meet.

## Step 1: Capture (fleeting notes and reference notes)

### Fleeting notes

- **What they are:** any passing thought, jotted down: ideas, reminders, questions, tasks, lists. Notes you've taken your whole life.
- **The key shift:** know that a fleeting note has not yet realized its potential. The process: have a thought, write it down, and know you'll need to transform it into a main note for it to become useful.
- **They never enter the zettelkasten.** They wait in the inbox until processed. **Process the inbox at least once a week.**
- **Journal users:** write "ZK" next to entries that feel "zettel worthy"; later, scan journals and turn the marked captures into main notes.
- **Not all fleeting notes go to heaven.** Tasks, reminders, shopping lists are not ideas and never become main notes. Depending on how much you capture, most fleeting notes will end up trashed or ignored. Practice non-attachment.
- Notes that repeatedly resist processing but still matter move to the **Sleeping folder**.

### Reference notes

- **What they are:** one long note per source (book, podcast, video, article, conversation, even your own journal), containing brief citations: page numbers (or timestamps/track numbers) plus a few words about what caught your attention. Luhmann's original method: on the front, page number + short phrase; bibliographic info on the back.
- **Where they live:** stored *inside* the zettelkasten, in the `Reference Notes` folder. They double as a record of everything you've read.
- **How much to write:** just enough to jog your memory later. No embellishment, no rules on how many captures per source. **Do not capture whole quotes routinely**; the text piles up and the note becomes unwieldy.
- **Borrowed books:** use separate reference notes (you lose the book, you keep the notes). **Books you own:** mark the margins and later pull what still interests you into a reference note.
- **Sources beyond books:** podcasts/videos use timestamps, music uses track numbers, conversations get creative citations (e.g., "conversation with X, date, topic").
- **Your own journals as sources:** give journals unique identifiers (volume numbers, dates, page numbers), then cite them in reference notes like any book.
- **Not all captures are gold.** You don't need to understand an idea's relevance to cite it. Reading is not note-taking; cite anything that catches your attention. Only some citations ever become main notes.

### Six things to consider while reading (his list)

1. Read with a question or problem in mind.
2. Capture ideas you disagree with (they're motivating).
3. Capture what *you* think about what the author thinks.
4. Capture someone else's interpretation when you're drawn to an idea but unsure why.
5. Don't forget fiction; plot, characters, scenes, even grammar can be mined.
6. Go wild. Anything that shows up while reading, external references, feelings, events, is grist.

### His marginalia system (for books you own)

- Boxes = terms to note
- Arrows = where the term is defined
- Underlines = passages to come back to
- Vertical lines = the passage is further explained below
- Smiley = resonates; frowny = disagree
- "ZK" = bring into the zettelkasten

### His five capture methods (pick what fits the medium)

1. Borrowed books → separate reference note (Luhmann's method).
2. Books he owns → marginalia, later turned into reference or main notes.
3. Morning spiritual reading → dated journal entries, "ZK" marks, later converted.
4. Articles → read-later app with notes, then imported as main notes.
5. Research → any medium, any method above.

## Step 2: Make main notes (the heart of the system)

### What's inside a main note

The minimum is **two components: a single idea, and a link to another idea in the zettelkasten.** If your notes contain nothing else, you're already on your way. Usually you'll add:

- **A title:** a declarative statement, not a descriptor. "Not all apples are edible" beats "Apples and edibility." Declarative titles tell you what the note says and make duplicates obvious.
- **A quote or reference** to where the idea comes from (paste the quote, or cite the reference note/page).
- **A record of where the idea has been used** in your writing (for reuse).
- **An alphanumeric ID** (see Step 4).

### The single idea (atomicity)

- Pare the thought down to its essentials. One idea per note, so it can freely associate with others.
- **"Atomic enough":** the fewest pieces necessary to be useful. Watch for **"but"** and **"however"** in your own note-making; whenever either appears, ask "should what comes next be a new note?" The answer is almost always yes.
- Splitting a complex idea into simpler ones creates more connection surface. Example from the book: "There's a discrepancy in the nutritional value of apples" becomes "Apples are good for your health" + "The nutritional value of apples is disputed"; each then links to far more trains of thought.

### How to create a main note from a fleeting note

1. Retype or copy the idea into a new file, in complete sentences, capturing the crux of the thought.
2. Consider whether it relates to a note already in the zettelkasten.
   - Unrelated: edit for readability, import it without links.
   - Related: rewrite it to speak directly to that note, or reference it and state why you're connecting.
3. Consider other ideas it might speak to; record those links **with context for each** (why the connection was made).
4. Add the alphanumeric ID to the title.
5. Give it a title that sums up the idea.

### From a reference-note capture

Same procedure, with one extra first step: retype/copy the quote into the new note first. That (1) reminds you where the idea came from, and (2) forces you to decide whether your idea restates the quote or was merely inspired by it, which determines how you cite it later.

### From your own writing

Blogs, articles, tweets, emails, forum comments: treat your past self like another writer. Copy the idea, consider relations, link, ID, title, store.

### On the spot

Making main notes generates more main notes. When a new idea arrives mid-note, open a new note, link it to the previous one, ID it, title it, store it. (His figures show chains of notes created one after another.)

### Questions and facts

- **Questions:** level them up by embedding them in a declaration. Instead of "Who am I?", write what you know about the question and why it matters. Unanswerable questions become the start of trains of thought.
- **Facts:** restate them in your own words (paraphrasing, not copying), give them context, and say something about them. "Servers connect to the Internet" becomes useful only when embedded in your own thinking ("Your home computer is not a server"). Then branch off into new notes.

### "In light of" vs "in spite of"

- **In light of:** developing a new note in direct response to notes already stored. This was Luhmann's way, and it's the ideal; it keeps the box "talking back."
- **In spite of:** developing an idea on its own, then retrofitting links afterward. This is how most modern, mobile lives work, and it's not wrong.
- Balance the two. Regularly coming back to the box, even briefly, builds familiarity so new ideas naturally develop in light of what's there.

### Contradicting notes

When a new idea contradicts an old one: **do not edit or delete the old note.** Create a new note with the counter-argument, state why the new idea wins, link it to the note it challenges. Wrong ideas, given context, become counter-arguments and show how your beliefs shifted.

### Notes don't need to be perfect

Luhmann called his zettelkasten a "septic tank"; he did not include only clarified notes. You don't need to know where a note will lead, include all the links, or represent your current thinking. The value gets decided later, in relation to other notes. Time per main note: anywhere from one to ten minutes. Spending longer defeats the purpose.

## Step 3: Connect ideas

- **It's all relationships.** A connection happens when one note cites another's ID or links to its file. "Previous" links continue a train of thought; "see" links jump across topics.
- **Making the first connection:** when a new idea reminds you of an existing one, find that note and link to it. Then think beyond the original context: a note on diverse soil composition could inform one on soil erosion, or serve as a metaphor for communities, or speak to diversity of life experience.
- **Context every "see" link.** State *why* the connection was made. "Link dumping" (dropping links with no explanation) assumes you'll remember later; you won't. Contextualized links also increase your understanding of the idea itself. ("Previous" links need no extra context; the note is the context.)
- **When starting out:** you have few notes; interrogate the idea instead. Is this note only about X? Must it only speak to other religion notes? His example: a Sufi saying about gift-giving became a note about parasocial relationships after questioning. Weird or forced connections are fine; more notes create more surface area for them to land.
- **Atomicity creates possibility:** the more atomic the idea, the more contexts it can be used in. Long, complex notes make writing into triage. Break ideas up now so you don't have to later.
- **Linking allows wild connections:** deliberately look for "internal possibilities for linkings which result in the unexpected" (Luhmann). Examples from his own box: apple branding ↔ corporate co-optation of subcultures.
- **Getting lost is a feature:** "the slip box provides combinatorial possibilities which were never planned, never preconceived." A note not connected to the network gets lost and forgotten. So: connect proactively, or the note dies.

## Step 4: Alphanumeric IDs (folgezettel)

IDs give every note a permanent identity, referenced regardless of content changes. His convention (the book's running example):

- **First note:** `1.1 Not all apples are considered edible`. Ideas are imported in the order they're created, not in semantic order. (Doto starts at 1.1, not 1, because 1 alone looks like a top-level category.)
- **Related idea (branch):** `1.1a Crab apples are often mistaken for cider apples` (a letter follows the parent).
- **Deeper branch:** `1.2a1 Macintosh apples entered commercial production in 1870` (letters then numbers).
- **New idea on the same general subject, not speaking to the last note:** next number: `1.2`, `1.3`, `1.4`.
- **An idea with nothing to do with previous ones:** next first number: `2.1 Skateboarding is not a crime`.
- **Same idea, different context:** the same skateboarding note could have been filed `1.4a` if it spoke to healthy eating.

Rules and clarifications:

- **IDs show a relationship exists, not what it means.** Folgezettel is not an outline, not a tree, not a hierarchy. IDs are **street addresses**: the occupant of 1A is not the sibling of the occupant of 1B.
- **There are no "occupied slots" and you never renumber.** Want a note between 1.1 and 1.1a? Choose any ID that adequately expresses the relationship. Context is everything; the same idea can begin a new area or extend an existing one depending on how you contextualize it.
- **Trains of thought can begin anywhere** in a section, not just at the first note. A branch can become the genesis of a new argument.
- **Section headings can be added later** (e.g., `1 APPLES`, `2 SPORTS AND SUBCULTURE`) purely as labels to help you locate areas. They are *not* categories and must never be used to decide where a new idea lands. That would kill bottom-up thinking.
- **Why bother (his three reasons):** (1) folgezettel gives a bird's-eye view of your initial connections without opening a single note; (2) it forces you to connect every new note, which starts trains of thought; (3) "eufriction" (good friction): manually applying IDs slows you down enough to think.
- Digital users can skip IDs, but he recommends keeping them.

## Step 5: Navigate the anarchy (high-level views)

The main compartment is a rhizome: every note is both an intersection and a departure point, no center, no hierarchy. To make sense of it without imposing structure, create "user interfaces" over it:

### Hub notes

- **What:** a note listing the entry points into the various trains of thought in a swollen area of the box. Each line: a topic phrase + the ID where that train starts. His example:

```
PKM and knowing .................... 3.4
Using systems for knowledge work ... 4.1
Spiritwork and pkm ................. 4.1b2
Functions of the zettelkasten ...... 4.3b
Luhmann's practices ................ 4.3c2
All things folgezettel ............. 4.3g
On-ramps, off-ramps, and friction .. 4.4
Project management ................. 4.5
```

- **How to create one:** pick a section where a theme has been taken in several directions; name the note after the topic; list the notes that initiate each train of thought, in the order they appear in your stack, with a brief description of where each leads.
- Hub notes point to where things are happening; they are not exhaustive lists and can live anywhere in the box.

### Structure notes

- **What:** a place where the contents of trains of thought get unpacked and developed semantically, like a table of contents or an outline.
- **How to create one:** title it with the topic; list whatever main notes speak to it (even just a rough list); then organize them into coherence, **giving context for each choice**; then search the box for other notes that speak to the developing theme and add them with context. Add new related notes over time.
- They double as rough drafts: with light editing, a well-developed structure note becomes an article. Missing or weak areas of your thinking show up here.

### Keyword index

- **Luhmann-style (austere, for serendipity):** a keyword plus a few IDs, no explanation. His example: `Naming .... 3.2; 4.3f`. Luhmann's own index gave at most four references per keyword in a box of 60,000 notes; the point is to enter the box and be surprised.
- **Database-style (explicit):** keywords with subtopics: `Relationships / parasocial .... 1.2b4`.
- Indexes can also point to emotions and experiences: "Ideas that make me laugh", "Ideas that arise in therapy", "Morning walking thoughts". Whatever helps you engage is a functioning index.

These three are lenses and guides only; they add no structure to the main notes themselves.

## Step 6: Write with your zettelkasten

### Let the box tell you what to write about

- **Clusters/lumps:** areas of increased connectivity. Long alphanumeric IDs show trains of thought you've built over time; scan them, open the interesting notes, copy the ideas into a working document.
- **Reference notes reveal real interests:** if most of your citations from a book on homesteading are about basket weaving, you're interested in basket weaving.
- **Structure notes scaffold drafts;** good main notes nudge you toward composition (title, main idea, contextualized links all become the skeleton of a piece).
- **Runaway ideas:** when an idea outgrows a note, do both: break it into main notes *and* write the article. Turn whatever didn't make it into the box into main notes afterward.

### Short-short content

Main notes convert directly into tweets/posts/polls. Responses and comments worth saving become new main notes: paste the comment, credit and link it, state your thinking, network it. Posting creates a feedback loop: output → engagement → input → output.

### Blog posts

1. Choose a note.
2. Rewrite the idea to fit your blog's tone; add information as needed.
3. Relate it to other posts and comments.
4. Consider connections for where the idea goes next.
5. Title and upload.

Don't spam readers with prepackaged main notes; engage with the responses.

### Articles, bottom-up (his 8 steps)

1. **Pick a note**, preferably one networked in a train of thought. Use IDs, index, hubs, structure notes to find it.
2. **Examine the connections.** Choose a train of thought and follow its sequence; if you dead-end, use the keyword index to mix things up.
3. **Create a new working file and import the ideas** (the raw material).
4. **Organize your thinking:** key points up front, expanding ideas below; look for ideas that both support and challenge your thinking.
5. **Edit and rewrite** what's in front of you to fit the piece; explain the connections; note gaps and write in what's needed.
6. **Decide on length** (short 500-2000 words, or substantial).
7. **Bring in more ideas if needed**, researching as necessary; return to steps 4-5.
8. **Finalize:** copyediting and restructuring first, then proofreading; a piece is close to done when you can read it through with almost no corrections.

Top-down and bottom-up oscillate during real writing; both are valid.

### Books

- **Start with abundance:** never begin from a blank page. Books are often curation of shorter works (he lists Nickel and Dimed, Bullshit Jobs, many spiritual classics). Your zettelkasten and past writing are the material; "the writing of your next book has already begun."
- **Outlines are living documents** that progress through four phases:
  1. **Brain dump:** list every subject and idea you can think of; quick, rough, phone-in-bed quality is fine.
  2. **Say more:** add subheadings and sentences to each section; pull text from the zettelkasten; this is where writing begins.
  3. **Break it up:** split the outline into separate chapter files.
  4. **Back-and-forth:** the outline guides the writing, then the writing forces the outline to change, round and round until writing dominates; the outline re-emerges at the end as the table of contents. This phase lasts months or years and is where the book gets tested.

### Don't let the box write for you

Your zettelkasten is a terrible writer. Notes were written outside any piece's context; you must reframe, rewrite, and embed them in the argument (Ahrens's advice). Writing from the box is **bricolage**, like Brian Wilson assembling "Good Vibrations" from modules: pieces are arranged and edited into cohesion. Editing guidelines: your first paragraph can probably go; lean into footnotes; not every note in a train belongs in the manuscript; what you don't use can be used elsewhere.

## Step 7: Maintain (his daily/weekly/monthly routines)

### Weekly

- **Process the inbox** at least once a week: fleeting notes become main notes, go to the Sleeping folder, or get trashed. Convert reference-note citations that still interest you into main notes.

### Every few months

- **Check the Sleeping folder** for ideas whose time has come.

### Daily (every workday): daily journal + creative logs

**The daily journal** is the central hub of his workday:

- Record the day's activities with thoughts about what transpired (**interstitial journaling**: between tasks, note what you completed, what you thought about it, what you're about to do, and what you think about doing it). Also record procrastination breaks; they reveal your workflow.
- Record conversations, tasks completed, bills paid, anything logged worth remembering.
- At the top: an **End of Day Check-In** with final thoughts and suggestions for tomorrow, written to your future self (write as if speaking to someone else).
- Format: one year-long running file, latest day at the top.

**Creative logs** track each piece of writing:

- Top of every log: two prompts, **"For others"** and **"For me"**, stating the purpose of the piece. Honesty required; "wanting to better understand my thinking" and "wanting to position myself as a thought leader" are both valid. If you can't articulate its value, the piece goes to a Sleeping folder.
- Notes section, three types of entries: (1) a record of what you did and when, (2) thoughts about what needs attention (tone, sources, paragraphs, quotes that don't fit), (3) suggestions on what to work on next session.
- Group creative logs by topic in single files so you can scan multiple projects at once.
- **The loop:** morning: read yesterday's journal, check the End of Day Check-In, open the recommended piece's creative log, then the document, then write. After the session: record in the creative log what you did and what's next, then journal the session's outcome and tomorrow's plan. Every workday until published.

### Writing with a deadline (task-focused project management)

For deadline work, five steps (works for any project):

1. **Define the project:** an outcome requiring more than one task, repeated check-ins, and an end date, titled in active form ("Design the cover", not "Cover design"). Big books break into milestone projects like "Write chapter 6 (due 10/6/23)".
2. **Brainstorm tasks:** list everything that might lead to completion, in any order. Add **"do" dates** (recommended days, flexible) vs **due dates** (fixed). Leave unsure dates blank; new tasks will emerge as you work.
3. **Gather resources:** from your zettelkasten, previously written articles, forum comments, social posts, old outlines, unread articles. Resources go to your task manager (unread links), the outline (early phases), or the document (later phases).
4. **Do only today's tasks.** Work multiple projects per day with variety: mix focused work (sourcing and organizing notes) with casual (adding headings) and creative (cover design). This fights fatigue and avoids blockages.
5. **Record progress in the creative log** (and the daily journal): what you did, where you left off, what's next. Most recent notes at the top.

There is no one system; scale the complexity to the project.

## Glossary: his terms vs the common ones

- **Fleeting note:** quick capture of a thought; never stored in the box. (Ahrens's term, kept for familiarity.)
- **Reference note = literature note = source note = bibliographic note:** one long note per source with brief citations; stored in the box.
- **Main note = permanent note:** single idea + links, stored in the box; the bulk of the zettelkasten.
- **Note-taking** = capturing (fleeting/reference). **Note-making** = distilling and contextualizing (main notes).
- **Train of thought** = a sequence of linked ideas (his word for "thread").
- **Folgezettel** = the alphanumeric follow-up-note ID system.
- **Slip box / zettelkasten:** interchangeable; paper and digital versions are treated the same, "compartments" and "folders" likewise.

## Beginner pitfalls (his "Things to Watch Out For", collected)

1. Not writing ideas down because you think you'll remember. You won't; capture.
2. Capturing without an inbox. Capture without a staging area makes for unhappy note-making.
3. Link dumping: links without context, assuming future-you remembers why.
4. Chasing "perfect" main notes or hoarding them. One idea plus one link is enough; the value is decided later, in relation.
5. Trying to find the "right spot" for a note, or thinking in categories. Keep thinking at the level of ideas.
6. Treating section headings as categories; adding structure top-down.
7. Expecting the box to write for you; shipping raw main notes as finished writing.
8. Importing your old notes into a fresh box.
9. Over-atomicizing into fragments or, the opposite, cramming divergent ideas into one long note.
10. Expecting productivity or quality guarantees: "maybe" on both. The box works only if you work the system, and it's no substitute for reading, feedback, and craft.

## Quick answers from his appendix

- A zettelkasten is **not** a second brain (second brains hold birthdays, directions, meeting notes; the box holds only ideas).
- Large is not better than small; value comes from how you work with contents.
- Main notes don't need to be "original"; connections reveal what's original.
- Luhmann's notes were "atomic-ish", concise but not constrained.
- How to find your notes: hub notes, structure notes, indexes, IDs, links, and (digital) search.
- Should you run two boxes (personal vs studies)? Only if you don't want those ideas to interact.
- Is it OK to take a break from the box? Yes.
